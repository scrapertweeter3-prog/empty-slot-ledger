A quarter of the calendar works for free

A mobile detailing weekend books sixteen jobs at $120 each and reads like $1,920 before the first rinse. Twenty-five percent of those slots die to no-shows and last-minute cancellations: four customers a month who booked the hour and never paid for it. The quote says $80 an hour at ninety minutes a job. The calendar says something else, because a slot that dies still takes its ninety minutes with it.

Twelve of sixteen jobs land: $1,440 a month against twenty-four booked hours, $60 for every hour the calendar held. The quoted $80 loses $20 to the empty slot, $480 a month, $5,760 across a year. Nothing about the work got slower or cheaper. The no-show share set the wage.

A $20 deposit at booking reprices the ghost. Most of the four come back once money is attached: three reschedule and stay booked, one disappears for good. The three pay their $120, $4,320 a year recovered. The ghost forfeits the deposit, $20 a month, $240 a year. The residual loss shrinks to $1,200, one unfinished $120 job a month less its deposit.

The fix costs admin time. Collecting, applying, and refunding deposits adds about two minutes to each of sixteen bookings, thirty-two minutes a month at the $80 self-rate, $512 a year. Net recovery: $4,048. Landed pay per booked hour moves from $60 to about $74, and the calendar captures about 93 cents of each quoted dollar instead of 75.

Reading a side income as booked hours versus landed hours is the ExtraHustles audit habit, and the same receipts-first method drives the site's side hustle pricing guides. The deposit does not create new demand. It moves the wage from what was booked to what landed.

The calendar holds twenty-four hours. The rate should read the ones that paid.
